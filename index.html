About

I grew up in Siliguri, a small town in West Bengal, where electricity was a privilege and education was a bigger one. When the power died at our house I went and found the fuse. Before that I was borrowing other kids' remote controlled cars, mostly so I could open them.

I am the third generation of entrepreneurs in my family and the first woman in it to be formally educated. Both of those shaped me about equally. It was a male dominated household in a town with firm opinions about what girls were for, and I was told often, by people who meant well, that girls are born to be married. My family got criticised for spending anything on my schooling. So I paid for it myself from the age of 11 with olympiad prize money and won a full scholarship to one of India's best boarding schools. That was the first thing I ever built: a way out that nobody could revoke.

Boarding school is where I found coding and stopped looking for anything else. At 11 and 12, working alongside university professors, I built Electroverter, a device that converts road traffic noise into electricity for homes in low income neighbourhoods. Loud, badly wired places have a resource in them that nobody was collecting. I sold electroverter and continued doing research on it at Harvard as a high schooler.

Then COVID sent me home and I watched school stop completely for children in my own city. I started Sahaay Education, which runs small government authorised schools in the underprivileged areas so those kids had somewhere to go. It is the work I was given the National Youth Icon Award for.

The one that changed things was Rail-Leela, a toy that teaches differently-abled children about India's culture and heritage. It won at state and national science fairs, and was picked in the top four out of roughly a hundred thousand projects in the ATL Marathon, the first project from my area ever selected. I was 15. It took me to a bootcamp and an internship at one of India's most renowned technological universities, and to pitching Rail-Leela to businesses.

Every award made the criticism louder, not quieter. So I stopped asking and got rebellious about it.

I started VisionWomxn so other girls would not have to fight it alone. Coding workshops, campaigns, and a rally of more than 1,500 women. Then I left for Toronto on a Dean's International Scholarship, at 17.

Toronto was my Hogwarts, and it is where I put a real toolkit under the instinct. I built Canada's first driverless formula car alongside my team and competed with it across North America and Europe. I am also a Cansbridge Fellow (2025), one of 15 Canadian students picked each year with under 2% acceptance. Past fellows include Thiel Fellows, Masason Fellows, YC and a16z-backed founders, and unicorn founders.

I invest too. I am an analyst at Northside Ventures, and have previously scouted for tier 1 VCs and angels.

Now I am building the thing all of it was pointing at. Robotics teams sit on petabytes of logs from real machines and select training data with recency windows, task labels, and intuition. Random subsampling is a real production baseline in 2026. I think physical AI is bottlenecked on data selection rather than architecture, and I am building the curation layer that fixes it.

The obsession that is not work is geothermal powered compute. Indonesia sits on a huge share of the world's geothermal reserves and uses a fraction of it while every AI company on earth scrambles for power. I spent a summer there standing at wellheads, then went to Taiwan and Canada to understand what a data center actually needs. Put the compute where the heat already is.

I also swim at national level, which is the only place I am reliably quiet.

Nothing I have built was built with the right parts. Please reach out, I try to answer everything.
